## It all started when...

Design Corps is a paid student consultancy at Dartmouth, run under the Dartmouth Design Initiative. Our job: study Dartmouth's 17 Living Learning Communities (themed residential houses) and tell senior campus leadership what was working and what wasn't.

I co-led a two-person research subgroup with one other student. In practice that meant I set the timeline, divided the work, chose the methodology, and owned the direction: where the research was actually headed. We reported up through a working group that answers to the SVP for Campus Life.

## How we scoped it

We covered 15 of the 17 communities. The research base:

- 23 individual semi-structured student interviews
- one group interview with 10 students
- one focus group with 8 students, which we organized ourselves
- 4 in-person tours of standalone residences
- benchmarking against residential models at 7 peer institutions

41 student voices in total, plus the outside comparison. Enough to see patterns repeat and to tell a real theme from a one-off.

## The directive

Early on, we ran a focus group entirely on our own initiative. We set it up end to end: recruited the eight students, wrote the questions, bought the food, and ran the session ourselves.

Afterward, leadership made a call: stop the individual interviews and wait for focus groups they would organize, using their own research process.

I understood the reasoning: they had their own way of running research, and focus groups add a kind of signal interviews don't. But we had just organized one ourselves, so I knew exactly how hard they are to pull off on a college campus. Getting eight students in one room at one time is a project in itself. Stopping our interviews cold, on the promise of sessions that didn't exist yet, felt like trading a bird in the hand for one that might never land.

So I asked for and got permission to keep running individual interviews while we waited. Not instead of their focus groups, alongside the wait for them.

Their focus groups never came together. Campus schedules are brutal, and nothing ended up on the calendar for us. If we'd stopped interviewing when the directive came down, we'd have had almost no data to show for the engagement. Because we kept going, our interviews became the foundation of everything we delivered.

## The outcome

As of our final report, none of the seven had been formally piloted. The research didn't translate into immediate action, and I don't want to dress that up.

What it did do mattered more than I expected. Leadership told us the findings closely matched concerns they already held but hadn't seen laid out with evidence behind them. That's the difference between a hunch and a documented case.

Then it showed up in writing.

## What this revealed about how I think

Two things.

First, complying with a directive and protecting the work are not always the same move, and when they diverge it's worth finding the version that does both. I didn't defy leadership. I did what they asked and kept a safety net running while their plan got a chance to work. When it didn't, the net mattered.

Second, the job with 41 interviews isn't collecting them, it's deciding which signals are real. Co-leading meant I couldn't just assert my read. I had to make the case for why a theme was a theme and not noise, to my research partner and then to leadership.
