#### The prompt

Our class challenge: use generative AI to meet an unmet need in the Dartmouth experience. We kept coming back to one idea, that wellness shouldn't be treated as separate from academics. Jed Foundation data showed Dartmouth's eating disorder rate sits above the national average, even though overall wellbeing is about on par. That gap stuck with us.

#### The pivot

We started with a fitness tracker. Then we took it to students in the library, and about 20 conversations later it was clear that wasn't the need. People weren't struggling to log workouts. They were dropping healthy eating right when school got hardest. We pivoted a few times before landing on BiteBuddy.

#### What we designed

Three features came straight out of the research:

- **It works around your schedule**, instead of asking you to work around it.
- **Meals are social.** You can share a meal with friends, because "I would use it if I could share meals with a friend" came up more than once.
- **Reminders adapt.** It learns what works for you and what doesn't.

We scoped the ethics before the features: privacy, how much control the AI gets, mental health, cultural sensitivity, and not pushing people to over-optimize. Given why we started, that part wasn't optional. Testers set the tone for us too: "Eating is not a reward, but a necessity."

#### What happened

We tested a working prototype with 12 students, and 10 said it would help their habits. One tester named the risk I'd go after next: "I predict a starting group that will start using it well but swipe off it after some time." We presented to faculty with a plan to partner with the Student Wellness Center. The project ended with the class, but it's where I learned to let a survey kill my first idea.
